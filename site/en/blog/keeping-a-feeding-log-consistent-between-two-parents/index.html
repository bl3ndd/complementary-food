Keeping a Feeding Log Consistent Between Two Parents

2026-09-07

A feeding diary kept by one person is usually fine. A feeding diary kept by two people is where things quietly fall apart — not because anyone is careless, but because two adults describe the same bowl of food in two different ways, and neither of them is wrong.

One parent writes "pear". The other writes "pear + oat, homemade, second time". Six weeks later you're scrolling back trying to work out whether the pear entries and the pear+oat entries were the same meal repeated, or two different things. That's the whole problem in one sentence.

Here's what actually helps.

Agree on what counts as one entry

Before you argue about wording, agree on granularity. Pick one and stick to it:

  • One entry per food. A meal with three components becomes three entries. More typing, but far easier to scan later when you want to know every single time a specific ingredient appeared.
  • One entry per meal. Everything eaten at once goes in a single note, with the components listed inside it. Faster to write, slower to search.

There's no right answer, but a diary that's half one and half the other is the one you'll stop trusting. We settled on one entry per food, because the whole point of the record was being able to search a single ingredient.

Standardise the words, not the sentences

You don't need a style guide. You need a shared vocabulary for the handful of things you'll write over and over.

  1. Use the plain ingredient name as the first word. carrot, not that orange purée. Whatever comes after is free text.
  2. Pick one spelling per food and never vary it. yoghurt or yogurt — decide once. Search doesn't forgive.
  3. Keep prep details in a fixed place. If you note steamed vs raw vs shop-bought, always put it right after the name: carrot, steamed.
  4. Don't put the reaction in the food name. carrot (bad night) pollutes your searches forever. Reactions belong in their own field or their own line.

Write the list of agreed words down somewhere both of you can see it. Ours lived in a pinned note for about three weeks, then we'd internalised it.

Decide who logs, per shift — not per meal

"Whoever feeds logs it" sounds obvious and fails constantly, because feeding is exactly when your hands are full. Two things worked better:

  • Log at the handoff. Whoever is going off duty writes down what happened during their block before they hand the baby over. It becomes part of the verbal handover you're already doing.
  • Nominate a nightly closer. One of you does a two-minute pass in the evening and fills the gaps. The other's job is just to tell them, not to type.

The second one is less pure but much more durable, especially if only one of you actually enjoys record-keeping. Somebody usually does.

Write down that you don't know

The most valuable habit we picked up: log the gaps explicitly. lunch — with grandma, unknown is worth ten times more than a blank space, because a blank space is ambiguous. Did nothing happen, or did nobody write it down?

Same for partial information. pasta, unsure if there was cheese is a real, honest record. If you later end up sitting in front of a pediatrician trying to reconstruct a week, those hedged entries are the ones that keep you from confidently telling them something false.

The format question, briefly

Two people editing the same log is where formats show their real cost:

  • Paper on the fridge is the fastest to write on and genuinely the best at getting both adults to participate — nobody has to unlock anything. It's the worst at searching back three months, and there's exactly one copy.
  • A shared notes document solves two-person editing, but everything becomes free text. No structure means no reliable search, and it drifts fastest.
  • A spreadsheet gives you columns, which forces consistency, and it filters beautifully. It's also miserable to fill in one-handed on a phone, which is when most entries actually get made.
  • A dedicated diary app gives you fixed fields, so both parents are answering the same questions. Pudding is one — free, offline, no account — and because it lives on the phone, the trade-off is that the record sits on one device rather than syncing between two.

That trade-off is real, and it's why the nightly-closer pattern works: one device holds the record, both parents feed it.

Review together once a week

Two minutes, out loud. Scroll the last seven days. You'll catch the double-entered meals, the day someone wrote veg instead of a name, and the entries that only make sense to the person who typed them. Fix them while you both still remember.

And if what you're looking at raises an actual question about your child — the record is what you bring to your pediatrician. The diary's job is to be accurate and complete. Interpreting it is somebody else's.
