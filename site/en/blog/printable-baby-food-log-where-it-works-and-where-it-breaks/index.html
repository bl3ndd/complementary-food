The Printable Baby Food Log: Where Paper Works and Where It Quietly Breaks

2026-09-21

A printable baby food log is the first thing most parents reach for. It makes sense: you find a grid, print two copies, tape one inside a kitchen cupboard, and start writing. No setup, no battery, no hunting for the right screen while holding a spoon in one hand.

I kept a paper log for the first several weeks and then quietly stopped. Not because paper is bad — it did some things better than anything on my phone — but because the specific job of a feeding record changed as the pages piled up. Here's what a printable log actually does well, and where it tends to break.

What a printable log is genuinely good at

It's visible. A sheet taped to the fridge gets filled in. An app you have to unlock, open, and navigate gets filled in later, which often means never. Physical visibility is the single biggest advantage paper has, and it's not a small one.

Anyone can use it. A grandparent who won't install anything, a partner who hates typing, a babysitter on their first afternoon — all of them can write "pear, small spoon, 4pm" on a line. No onboarding.

It's fast. Writing three words takes less time than typing them. No autocorrect turning a food name into something else.

It survives. No sync errors, no phone in the other room, no accidental deletion.

If your whole goal is this week, a printed sheet is hard to beat.

What to actually put on the page

Most printable templates you find online have too many columns. Half of them go unfilled by day three, and empty columns make the whole log feel like a failure. Keep it to what you'll really write:

  1. Date — one row per day, or one sheet per day if you write a lot.
  2. What was offered — the food name, plainly. Write the specific thing: "oatmeal + pear" not "breakfast."
  3. New or repeat — a single check mark. This is the column you'll care about most later.
  4. What you noticed — skin, sleep, mood, stool, how much came back out of the mouth. Two or three words is fine.
  5. Who recorded it — initials. Saves a lot of guessing about whose "he was fussy" that was.

Leave a wide right-hand margin for the thing you didn't expect to note. That margin ends up being the most useful part of the sheet.

What not to build into the template: anything that implies a target. No "planned foods for this week," no portion goals, no checkboxes for what should have been introduced. A log records what happened. Questions about what or when to offer anything belong with your pediatrician, not a grid you printed at home.

Where the paper log breaks

It breaks in four predictable places.

Searching back

The whole point of keeping a record is that one day you'll need to look something up — a rash, a rough night, an odd stool — and ask when did we first give this, and what else was new around then? With paper, that means physically reading pages. Forty sheets in, finding "every day that had lentils on it" is a twenty-minute job, and you'll miss some.

Two people, one sheet

Paper works for two parents only when both are standing in the same kitchen. Daycare pickup notes, a grandparent's afternoon, a weekend at the in-laws — all of that arrives as verbal reports that never make it onto the page. The sheet develops holes exactly where the unfamiliar foods were.

Photos

A photo of the plate tells you the texture, the actual mix, and roughly how much came back. A photo of a reaction tells a doctor far more than your adjective for it. Paper can't hold either, so your photos live in a camera roll of 4,000 images with no connection to the line that says "tried squash."

Handing it to someone

A stack of curling sheets with coffee on them is hard for a pediatrician to read in the two minutes they have. A dated, ordered summary is easy.

A workable hybrid

You don't have to choose cleanly. What worked for me:

  • Keep the printed sheet on the fridge as the capture surface — anyone can scribble on it in the moment.
  • Once a day, at a fixed moment, transfer the lines into the searchable record on your phone, attaching any photos from that day. It takes about a minute and it's when I remembered details I'd otherwise have lost.
  • Photograph or bin the paper sheet afterwards. Don't keep two authoritative records; one of them will be wrong.

This is roughly the shape Pudding is built around: a plain diary of what was given, on which day, and what you noticed after — free, offline, no account, so it works in a kitchen with bad signal and doesn't turn your child's food history into someone else's data. It won't tell you what to feed; that's not its job, and it's not this article's either.

If you're starting today, print the sheet. It'll get you writing, which is the hard part. Just plan for the moment — usually somewhere around the second month — when you need to look backwards, and the pages stop answering.
