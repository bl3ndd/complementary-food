Feeding Notes for Daycare or Grandparents: Keeping the Record Intact When Someone Else Feeds

2026-10-19

The first time someone else fed my daughter a full meal without me, I got back a report that said: "She ate well!"

That sentence is lovely and completely useless three weeks later, when you are sitting in a waiting room trying to remember what she had on a Tuesday.

The problem is not that grandparents and daycare staff are careless. The problem is that they are not keeping your record. They are feeding a child, cleaning up, and moving on with their day. If you want the information, you have to make writing it down take less effort than remembering it.

Here is what actually worked for us.

Decide what you need back — and nothing more

The instinct is to hand over a detailed form. Resist it. Every extra field is one more reason the whole thing gets skipped.

In practice, the notes that survive a handoff are short. Ours ended up as four things:

  • What — the name of the food, written plainly ("oatmeal", "pear", "that orange puree in the fridge")
  • When — a rough time of day is fine, exact clock time is not required
  • New or not — whether this was something the child had not had before
  • Anything odd — refusals, a rash, unusual fussiness, anything that made the adult pause

That last one is the most valuable and the hardest to get. People self-censor. A grandparent who thinks they might be imagining something will usually say nothing rather than worry you. Tell them explicitly: write it down even if you think it's nothing, I'm just keeping a list. Framing it as a list rather than a concern makes people honest.

If anything in those notes makes you uneasy, that is a question for your pediatrician — the notes exist so you can describe it accurately, not so you can interpret it yourself.

Make the recording format match the person

This is where most systems break. You cannot give everyone the same tool.

Daycare usually already has its own system — a daily sheet, a whiteboard, an end-of-day summary. Do not fight it. Ask what they already record and whether you can get it in writing rather than verbally at pickup. Verbal reports evaporate on the drive home.

Grandparents vary enormously. Some will happily type into a phone. Others will not, ever, and asking them to is a quiet source of friction. A sticky note on the fridge works. A scrap of paper in the diaper bag works. The format does not matter as long as it physically comes back to you.

A sitter is usually the easiest: they expect to be given a system and will follow one.

For people who do not want to install or learn anything, the lowest-friction option I found was a text message. One message, sent when they remember, with whatever they can recall. No app, no login, no form. I transcribe it later.

Build a daily reconciliation habit

Here is the part nobody tells you: the handoff notes are raw material, not the record. They only become useful once they are merged into one place.

What I do, most evenings, takes about ninety seconds:

  1. Check for the day's incoming notes — text message, daycare sheet, scrap of paper.
  2. Enter each item into the diary alongside whatever we fed at home.
  3. Mark anything that came secondhand, so I know later that I did not see it myself.
  4. Throw the scrap away.

That third step matters more than it sounds. Months later, when you are reconstructing a week, there is a real difference between "I watched her eat this" and "someone told me she ate this." A short tag in the note — via daycare, grandma — preserves that distinction without any extra system.

I keep ours in Pudding, partly because it is free and has no account to set up, and mostly because it works offline — I do this on the sofa, at the end of a long day, and it never asks me to do anything except type the food and tap save. Any tool that lets you add an entry in under a minute will do the job. The habit is the thing, not the app.

Write the handoff instruction once, reuse it forever

Do not re-explain your system every time. Write it once — a few lines in a note on your phone — and send the same text to whoever is feeding your child that day.

Something like: "If you can, text me what she ate and roughly when. If anything seemed off — didn't want it, scratched at anything, unusual mood — tell me even if it seems trivial. I'm just keeping a running list."

That is it. No form, no app, no training.

What this buys you

The payoff is not visible for weeks. Then something comes up, you open the diary, and the days other people fed your child are not blank. There is no gap where you have to say "she was at daycare, I don't know."

A record with holes in it still answers questions — it just answers them less confidently. Closing the handoff gap is the cheapest way to make the whole history more trustworthy, and it costs you about ninety seconds a night.
