Notes App vs Dedicated Feeding Diary: What Each One Costs You Later

2026-09-28

Almost everyone starts in the notes app. It's already on the phone, it's already unlocked in your hand, and the first entry takes four seconds: pear, half a spoon, fine. That's a good start — a real record beats a perfect system that you never open.

The question isn't which format is better. It's which one still works in month five, when you need to find something you wrote in month two and can't remember the word you used for it. Here's what each format actually costs, from someone who has kept both.

What a general notes app does well

  • Zero setup. No fields to configure, no structure to decide on.
  • Free-form. You can write a paragraph about a weird evening, or three words, and nothing complains.
  • Already synced. If you use the same account across devices, your note is on the iPad too.
  • Fast capture. Dictation, widgets, lock-screen shortcuts — the note is one tap deep.

For the first week or two this is genuinely enough. If your whole record is going to be twelve entries, stop reading and keep using the notes app.

Where the notes app starts to hurt

It breaks in four specific places, and always in the same order.

1. One long note becomes unreadable

Most people write into a single note and append to the bottom. By week six it's a wall of text with inconsistent dates (Tue, 14/3, today, and one entry with no date at all because you were holding a baby). Scrolling to find something means reading, not searching.

The fix people try is one note per day. Then you have ninety notes with near-identical titles and the search results are useless because every note matches.

2. Search only finds the word you happened to type

Search in a notes app is text search. It finds what you wrote, exactly as you wrote it. If you typed courgette in March and zucchini in May, those are two different foods as far as your phone is concerned. Same for yoghurt and yogurt, or egg and scrambled.

A dedicated diary has a food as a thing, not a string of characters. You pick it once and every mention of it links together, which means the question "have we ever had this before, and what happened?" is one tap instead of an archaeology project.

3. Photos and text drift apart

You take the photo in the camera app because that's where the camera is. Now the photo is in the library, sorted by time, and the note is in the notes app, sorted by whenever you got round to typing it. Reuniting them later means matching timestamps by hand. A diary that attaches the photo to the entry keeps them together permanently — and if you want to know what that looks like in practice, we wrote about organising photos, portions and notes so the history stays findable.

4. Two people editing the same note

Shared notes mostly work, until two adults type at the same time and one version wins, or your partner uses a different device account and simply never sees it. Then you have two partial records and no way to know which one is complete. Reconciling them from memory a month later is the exact situation you started a diary to avoid.

What a dedicated diary gives up in exchange

Be fair to the notes app. A purpose-built diary costs you:

  • Flexibility. Structured fields mean there are things they don't have a box for. A good diary still leaves a free-text note; use it.
  • A second place to look. One more app, one more habit.
  • The setup minute. You do have to install it and make a first entry.

That's the trade. Structure in exchange for a bit of freedom.

How to decide, concretely

Ask yourself these three questions:

  1. **Do I ever expect to look backwards?** If the record only exists to be written, notes are fine. If you expect to answer "when did we first try this?", you need something searchable by food, not by text.
  2. Is more than one adult writing? Two parents, a grandparent, daycare handover — the more hands, the more you need consistent fields instead of free prose.
  3. Will anything leave the app? If you might want to hand a clean summary to your pediatrician, check the format can produce one. Retyping three months of notes the night before an appointment is a bad evening.

If you're switching

Don't transcribe everything. Really.

  • Start the new diary today, properly, with real entries.
  • Keep the old note. Don't delete it, don't rewrite it.
  • Copy across only the first appearances of foods and anything that stood out. That's the part you'll actually want to look up.
  • Put one line at the top of the new record: "Entries before [date] are in my old note." Future you will thank present you.
