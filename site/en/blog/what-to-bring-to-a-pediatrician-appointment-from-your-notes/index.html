What to Bring to a Pediatrician Appointment From Your Own Notes

2026-09-14

An appointment is short. Your feeding diary, if you've been keeping one for a while, is not. Scrolling through weeks of entries in a waiting room with a baby on your lap is how you end up saying "I think it was maybe two weeks ago? Possibly a Tuesday?" — which is exactly the situation a diary was supposed to prevent.

What helps is doing ten minutes of preparation the night before. Not writing anything new: just pulling the relevant part of what you already have into something you can hand over or read off.

Start by narrowing the window

Before you sort anything, decide what the appointment is actually about. "Something happened on Saturday and I want to ask about it" is a different search than "we have a routine check-up and I'd like to mention how the last month has gone."

Once you know the question, you know the date range. For a one-off event, most parents end up looking at the days immediately around it. For a general conversation, a broader stretch is more useful than a single day. Everything outside that window can stay where it is — you don't need to summarise four months of eating to ask about one afternoon.

Build a one-page summary

The thing worth walking in with is short and dated. A single page, or one screen, containing:

  • The dates, written out properly. "14 March" beats "a couple of weeks ago" every time.
  • What was given, by name, including anything mixed in. Brand and preparation matter more than you'd think — "tomato" and "jarred pasta sauce" are not the same line in a diary.
  • Whether it was new or repeated, and if repeated, when the earlier times were.
  • What you observed, in plain description: what you saw, where, how long it lasted, what it looked like. Photos help here more than adjectives.
  • The gap in time between eating and whatever you noticed. Even "about an hour, I didn't check the clock" is more useful than nothing.
  • Your actual questions, written down. Three at most, in priority order, because you will forget the third one.

That's the document. It doesn't need conclusions. You are the person who was there; the interpretation is the appointment's job, and guessing on paper mostly makes the guess harder to walk back.

Write observations, not diagnoses

This is the habit that pays off most. In your notes, "red blotchy patch on both cheeks, gone by bath time" is worth far more than "reaction." The first can be discussed. The second is a conclusion you've already drawn, and once it's in your notes you tend to stop questioning it.

The same goes for describing a bad evening. "Refused the spoon after two bites, arched back, calmed down when picked up" gives a much clearer picture than "hated it." If you're unsure which detail matters, write both — sorting is cheaper than remembering.

Decide the format before you leave the house

How you deliver this matters more than it should, because the reality is a shared screen in a small room.

  • Printed on paper. Easiest to hand over, easiest to annotate, impossible to search. Fine for a single summarised page.
  • Read from your phone. Works if you've prepared the summary. Doesn't work if you're scrolling a long diary looking for a date.
  • A PDF export. The most portable option: it can be shown, emailed, or printed at reception. Pudding can export your diary to PDF, which means you can bring the full dated history along without handing over your phone or reading it out entry by entry.
  • Screenshots in your camera roll. Surprisingly effective as a backup. Take shots of the relevant entries the night before, so they're the newest photos and you're not hunting.

If you keep your diary in an app, check beforehand that you can actually get the data out in some shareable form. A record you can only see inside one screen is a record you'll end up retyping.

Bring the photos, but pick them first

Photos of a plate or of something you noticed on skin are genuinely useful, and they're also the hardest thing to find under pressure. Camera rolls are ordered by time, not by relevance, and the photo you want is somewhere between four hundred others.

If your diary stores photos attached to the entry — the way Pudding does, free and offline, with no account to log into first — the picture is already next to the date and the food name, which is the only context that makes it mean anything. If your photos live in your camera roll, note the timestamp in your written entry so you can find them again.

Write down what was said, in the same place

The last step happens after. Add a dated entry recording what you asked and what you were told, in the same diary as everything else. Otherwise it lives in memory, gets softer each week, and by the next appointment you're reconstructing your own last appointment.

A feeding diary isn't only a record of food. It's a record of the questions you've already asked — and that's the part that stops you asking them twice.
